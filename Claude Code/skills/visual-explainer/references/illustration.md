# Editorial illustration

Read this branch when the requested result is a drawing, conceptual image,
poster, or editorial illustration. A polished flowchart is not automatically
an illustration. Start with the subject and a visual idea, then find the image
that carries it within the bundled design system.

## Find an image, not a diagram skin

Choose a visible situation or transformation that embodies the idea. It may
use a recognizable object, human figure, organic silhouette, cutaway, field of
marks, material-like layers, or purely geometric relationships. A useful
metaphor has a reason for its important shapes and their interaction; the
reason must precede the caption.

Prefer one strong focal construction to several small unrelated symbols.
Fine construction lines, repetition, occlusion, scale contrast, and negative
space can explain what a label-heavy diagram would name. Each should have a
job: repeated layers can reveal structure, for example, but a dense field of
dots alone does not explain memory, intelligence, or quality.

Do not build every illustration as a rectangle containing a small centered
icon. Choose framing, visual mass, empty space, and cropping for the subject.
A series can mix compositions while retaining the palette and type roles.
Avoid random ornament and generic technical-looking detail.

## Make useful alternatives

For a requested set, vary the image's construction, not only its subject label.
Recognizable and abstract imagery, solid masses and open contours, a close-up
and an expanded view can offer meaningfully different readings. These are
options, not a mandatory catalog or a fixed number of variants. Do not imitate
the distinctive layout or individual drawings of a reference sheet.

Translate aesthetic feedback into the relevant choice: a request for richer
drawings is not a request to change the palette, abandon the idea, or repeat
the reference's subject. Preserve separately approved style and content.

## Execute and inspect

Construct the drawing directly in SVG: Bezier contours for a silhouette,
overlapping shapes and masks for interweaving, repeated paths for layers or
cross-sections, and negative space for cuts and openings. Choose the technique
for the idea rather than using one construction for every subject. These are
vector-authoring options, not external image-generation steps or fixed templates.
Inspect a rough SVG at the destination size before detailing it. With visual
references, compare silhouette, relative scale, cropping, density, rhythm, and
line character; matching the palette alone is insufficient. Can a viewer
distinguish the important forms and understand their relationship without the
caption supplying a missing mechanism? Revise the construction before polishing
a weak idea. For a series, compare the actual drawings together to catch repeated
compositions disguised by different labels.

Then inspect detail: keep calculated curves smooth at their joins, make overlaps
and openings physically legible, and preserve editable type and consistent fills.
Check for clipping, accidental gradients, and background drift. An interesting
silhouette is insufficient if its apparent mechanism contradicts the message.
Do not invent a technical or scientific explanation for a purely metaphorical form.

Keep concept studies distinct from final artwork. A vector master must contain
the actual drawing, not an embedded bitmap. Preserve sources and identify any
remaining style deviation.
