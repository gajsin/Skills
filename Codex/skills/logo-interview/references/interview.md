# Interview and concept grounding

## Brief lenses

Reuse answered topics. The brief core is stable; useful questions depend on the current gaps.

| Lens | Useful question | Decision it informs |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | What is this project, and what should people recognize or feel? | What the mark needs to do |
| Name | What exact spelling, script, or abbreviation is settled? | Naming and lettering possibilities |
| Context | Who encounters it, where, and with what prior knowledge? | Intended signal and audience assumptions |
| Existing identity | What exists, works, or must survive? | New identity versus continuity |
| Applications | Where must it work first? | Relevant sizes, media, and reproduction limits |
| Character | What should it feel like, and what do those words mean here? | Qualities specific enough to explore |
| Taste | What attracts or repels you in this example? Which part? | Form, color, type, rhythm, or association |
| Limits and decisions | What is required, forbidden, open, and who chooses? | Constraints and approval criteria |

Research available factual context yourself. Explore history or a concrete episode only when it could clarify the concept; do not require a biography or invent market evidence.

## Optional character exercises

When character is unclear, offer person and animal analogies separately, with free answers. Translate the prompts naturally into the user's language:

- "If this logo were a person, what kind of person would it be?"
- "If it were an animal, which one, and which of its qualities feel right?"

Ask which qualities matter. Associations with movement, posture, clothing, appearance, or proportions suggest hypotheses; do not assign personality from physique, age, or species. Confirm whether an analogy describes character or an intended subject in the mark.

Skip answered, declined, or unhelpful exercises. Other metaphors can clarify a specific uncertainty; do not collect an inventory or treat analogies as a complete brief or psychological test.

## Clarify preferences

Use concrete examples when adjectives are ambiguous. Compare genuinely different images, explore a boundary such as "too formal," or let the user group examples as closer, farther, and uncertain. Separate enjoying an image from its suitability for this project.

Reflect interpretations with evidence: "You highlighted X and rejected Y; I read that as Z." Keep this tentative until confirmed or delegated. "Interesting" does not necessarily mean "liked." Apparent contradictions may be combinations worth exploring. Explain a real tradeoff before asking which property takes priority.

## Build a concept

Distinguish the identifying basis (name, subject, identity, or genuine association), the desired character, and the visible mechanism expressing it. A provisional rationale can be simple: what identifies the owner, which qualities matter, and how the construction supports them. Do not invent a hidden story for each cut or imply that every mark needs a literal subject. A loose word-to-object mapping alone does not establish relevance.

Before presenting a candidate, judge:

- **Fit:** how does its idea serve this identity and purpose?
- **Reading:** what should be recognizable, and what competing interpretations or category signals are plausible?
- **Expression:** does the shown form support the intended character and desired distinctiveness?

Apply these checks yourself; they do not prove audience reception or originality. Improve or discard weak candidates. When literal recognition matters, explanation cannot replace the visible cue.

Explain the candidate's basis, visible choices, and uncertainty before detailed refinement; early sketches can test a provisional idea. A permitted letter or object remains a possibility until selected. Use the brief's decision levels to distinguish liking a property from choosing a construction.
